    Semi-Supervised Semantic Segmentation Using Unreliable Pseudo-Labels

    Abstract

    Github Haochen-Wang409 (U2PL)

    The crux (main point) of semi-supervised (partially labeled) semantic segmentation (dividing an image into meaningful parts) is to assign adequate (enough, sufficient) pseudo-labels (fake labels) to the pixels of unlabeled images.

    A common practice is to select the highly confident (sure) predictions (outputs of a model) as the pseudo ground-truth(data or labels that are not real but are assumed to be correct for training or evaluation purposes), but it leads to a problem that most pixels may be left unused due to their unreliability.

    We argue (To express different opinions or give reasons for something) that every pixel matters (is important) to the model training, even its prediction is ambiguous (not clear).

    Intuitively, an unreliable prediction may get confused among the top classes (categories with the highest probabilities), however, it should be confident about the pixel not belonging to the remaining (other) classes.

    Hence (so), such a pixel can be convincingly (in a way that makes others believe) treated(the act or behave towards someone or something in a particular way) as a negative sample to those most unlikely categories.

    Based on this insight (idea), we develop an effective (successful) pipeline (process) to make sufficient (enough) use of unlabeled data.

    Concretely (specifically), we separate reliable and unreliable pixels via the entropy (a measure of uncertainty) of predictions, push each unreliable pixel to a category-wise (a list for each group) queue that consists of negative samples, and manage to train the model with all candidate (possible) pixels.

    Considering the training evolution (change over time), where the prediction becomes more and more accurate (correct), we adaptively ( In a way that changes to fit new conditions) adjust the threshold (limit) for the reliable-unreliable partition (division).

    Experimental (Related to tests or trials to discover something new) results on various benchmarks (tests) and training settings demonstrate (show) the superiority (better quality) of our approach over the state-of-the-art (best) alternatives (choices).

    1. Introduction

    Semantic segmentation (dividing an image into meaningful parts) is a fundamental (basic) task in the computer vision (field of AI that processes images) field, and has been significantly (greatly) advanced along with the rise of deep neural networks (mathematical models inspired by the human brain) [7,30,36,47]. Existing (currently available) supervised (fully labeled) approaches rely on large-scale annotated (labeled) data, which can be too costly (expensive) to acquire (obtain) in practice. To alleviate (reduce) this problem, many attempts [1, 4, 11, 17, 23, 34, 44, 49] have been made towards semi-supervised (partially labeled) semantic segmentation, which learns a model with only a few labeled samples and numerous (many) unlabeled ones. Under such a setting, how to adequately (sufficiently) leverage (utilize) the unlabeled data becomes critical (important).

    Figure 1. Category-wise (group-specific) performance and statistics on the number of pixels with reliable and unreliable predictions. The model is trained using 732 labeled images on PASCAL VOC 2012 [16] and evaluated on the remaining 9,850 images.

    A typical (common) solution is to assign pseudo-labels (fake labels) to the pixels without annotations (labels). Concretely (specifically), given an unlabeled image, prior arts [28,42] borrow predictions (outputs of a model) from the model trained on labeled data, and use the pixel-wise (per pixel) prediction as the “ground-truth” (true label) to in turn boost (improve) the supervised model. To mitigate (reduce) the problem of confirmation bias (systematic error) [2], where the model may suffer from incorrect pseudo-labels, existing (currently available) approaches propose to filter the predictions with their confidence (certainty) scores [43, 44, 51, 52]. In other words, only the highly confident predictions are used as the pseudo-labels, while the ambiguous (unclear) ones are discarded (ignored). However, one potential (possible) problem caused by only using reliable predictions is that some pixels may never be learned in the entire training process. For example, if the model cannot satisfyingly (adequately) predict some certain class (e.g., chair in Fig. 1), it becomes difficult to assign accurate (correct) pseudo-labels to the pixels regarding such a class, which may lead to insufficient (not enough) and categorically (group-wise) imbalanced (uneven) training. From this perspective (viewpoint), we argue (say strongly) that, to make full use of the unlabeled data, every pixel should be properly (appropriately) utilized (used). As discussed above, directly using the unreliable predictions as the pseudo-labels will cause performance degradation (decline) [2]. In this paper, we propose an alternative (different) way of Using Unreliable Pseudo-Labels. We call our framework as U^2 PL. First, we observe (notice) that an unreliable prediction usually gets confused among only a few classes instead of all classes. Taking Fig. 2 as an instance (example), the pixel with a white cross receives similar probabilities on class motorbike and person, but the model is pretty sure about this pixel not belonging to class car and train. Based on this observation (finding), we reconsider (rethink) the confusing (unclear) pixels as the negative samples to those unlikely (least probable) categories. Specifically (in detail), after getting the prediction from an unlabeled image, we employ (use) the per-pixel entropy (a measure of uncertainty) as the metric (measure) (see Fig. 2) to separate all pixels into two groups, i.e., a reliable one and an unreliable one. All reliable predictions are used to derive (generate) positive pseudo-labels, while the pixels with unreliable predictions are pushed into a memory bank (storage), which is full of negative samples. To avoid all negative pseudo-labels only coming from a subset (part) of categories, we employ (use) a queue (a data structure) for each category. Such a design ensures (guarantees) that the number of negative samples for each class is balanced (even). Meanwhile (at the same time), considering that the quality of pseudo-labels becomes higher along with the model becoming more and more accurate (correct), we come up with a strategy to adaptively (in a way that changes) adjust the threshold (limit) for the partition (division) of reliable and unreliable pixels. We evaluate (assess) the proposed U^2 PL on PASCAL VOC 2012 [16] and Cityscapes [12] under a wide range of training settings, where our approach surpasses (outperforms) the state-of-the-art (best) competitors. Furthermore (in addition), through visualizing (making visible) the segmentation results, we find that our method achieves much better performance on those ambiguous (unclear) regions (areas) (e.g., the border between different objects), thanks to our adequate (sufficient) use of the unreliable pseudo-labels.

    2. Related Work

    Semi-Supervised Learning has two typical (common) paradigms (models): consistency regularization (making predictions consistent) [3, 17, 34, 37, 43] and entropy minimization (reducing uncertainty) [4, 18]. Recently (lately), a more intuitive (natural) but effective (successful) framework, self-training (training a model on its own predictions) [28], has become the mainstream (most common). Several methods [17, 44, 45] utilize (use) strong data augmentation (enhancement) such as CutOut [15], CutMix [46], and ClassMix [32] based on self-training. However, these methods do not pay much attention to the characteristics (features) of semantic segmentation, while our method focuses on those

    unreliable (not trustworthy) pixels which will be filtered out by most of self-training based methods [35, 44, 45]. Pseudo-Labeling (assigning fake labels) is applied to prevent overfitting (fitting too closely) to incorrect pseudo-labels when generating predictions of input images from the teacher network [2, 28]. FixMatch [38] utilizes (uses) a confidence threshold (limit) to select reliable pseudo-labels. UPS [35], a method based on FixMatch [38], takes model uncertainty (doubt) and data uncertainty into consideration. However, in semi-supervised semantic segmentation, our experiments show including unreliable pixels into training can boost (improve) performance. Model Uncertainty in computer vision (field of AI that processes images) is mostly measured by Bayesian deep learning approaches [14, 25, 31]. In our settings, we do not focus on how to measure uncertainty. We simply use the entropy (a measure of uncertainty) of pixel-wise probability distribution to be the metric (measure). Contrastive Learning (learning by comparing) is applied by many successful works in self-supervised learning [9, 10, 19]. In semantic segmentation, contrastive learning has become a promising (hopeful) new paradigm (model) [1, 29, 41, 48, 50]. However, these methods ignore the common false negative samples in semi-supervised segmentation, and unreliable pixels may be wrongly pushed away in contrastive loss. Discriminating (distinguishing) the unlikely (least probable) categories of unreliable pixels can address this problem. Negative Learning (learning from negative examples) aims at decreasing the risk of incorrect information by lowering the probability of negative samples [26, 27, 35, 40], but those negative samples are selected with high confidence (certainty). In other words, these methods still utilize (use) pixels with reliable predictions. By contrast (in comparison), we propose to make sufficient (enough) use of those unreliable predictions for learning instead of filtering them out.

    3. Method

    In this section, we establish (set up) our problem mathematically and give an overview of our proposed method in Sec. 3.1. first. Our strategies about filtering reliable pseudo-labels are introduced in Sec. 3.2. Finally, we describe how to use unreliable pseudo-labels in Sec. 3.3.

    3.1. Overview

    Given a labeled set \( D_l = \{(x_l^i, y_l^i)\}_{i=1}^{N_l} \) and a much larger unlabeled set \( D_u = \{x_u^i\}_{i=1}^{N_u} \), our goal is to train a semantic segmentation model by leveraging (utilizing) both a large amount of unlabeled data and a smaller set of labeled data. Fig. 3 gives an overview of U^2 PL, which follows the typical (common) self-training framework with two models of the same architecture, named teacher and student respectively. The two models differ only when updating their weights. The student model’s weights \( \theta_s \) are updated consistent with the common practice and the teacher model’s weights \( \theta_t \) are exponential moving average (EMA) updated by the student model’s weights. Each model consists of a CNN-based encoder \( h \), a decoder with a segmentation head \( f \), and a representation head \( g \). At each training step, we equally sample \( B \) labeled images \( B_l \) and \( B \) unlabeled images \( B_u \). For every labeled image, our goal is to minimize the standard cross-entropy loss in Eq. (2). As for each unlabeled image, we first take it into the teacher model and get predictions. Then, based on pixel-level entropy (a measure of uncertainty), we ignore unreliable pixel-level pseudo-labels when computing unsupervised loss in Eq. (3). This part will be introduced in section Sec. 3.2 in detail. Finally, we use the contrastive loss (loss from comparing) to make full use of the unreliable pixels excluded in the unsupervised loss, which will be introduced in Sec. 3.3. Our optimization target is to minimize the overall loss, which can be formulated (expressed) as:

    \[ L = L_s + \lambda_u L_u + \lambda_c L_c, (1) \]

    where \( L_s \) and \( L_u \) represent supervised loss and unsupervised loss applied on labeled images and unlabeled images respectively, and \( L_c \) is the contrastive loss to make full use of unreliable pseudo-labels. \( \lambda_u \) and \( \lambda_c \) are weights of unsupervised loss and contrastive loss respectively. Both \( L_s \) and \( L_u \) are cross-entropy (CE) loss:

    \[ L_s = \frac{1}{|B_l|} \sum_{(x_l^i, y_l^i) \in B_l} \ell_{ce}(f \circ h(x_l^i; \theta), y_l^i), (2) \]
    \[ L_u = \frac{1}{|B_u|} \sum_{x_u^i \in B_u} \ell_{ce}(f \circ h(x_u^i; \theta), \hat{y}_u^i), (3)\]

    where \( y_l^i \) represents the hand-annotated mask label for the \( i \)-th labeled image, and \( \hat{y}_u^i \) is the pseudo-label for the \( i \)-th unlabeled image. \( f \circ h \) is the composition function of \( h \) and \( f \), which means the images are first fed into \( h \) and then \( f \) to get segmentation results. \( L_c \) is the pixel-level InfoNCE [33] loss defined as:

    \[ L_c = -\frac{1}{C \times M} \sum_{c=0}^{C-1} \sum_{i=1}^{M} \log \frac{e^{\langle z_c^i, z_c^{+i} \rangle / \tau}}{e^{\langle z_c^i, z_c^{+i} \rangle / \tau} + \sum_{j=1}^{N} e^{\langle z_c^i, z_c^{-ij} \rangle / \tau}}, (4)\]

    where \( M \) is the total number of anchor pixels, and \( z_c^i \) denotes the representation of the \( i \)-th anchor of class \( c \). Each anchor pixel is followed with a positive sample and \( N \) negative samples, whose representations are \( z_c^{+i} \) and \( z_c^{-ij} \) respectively. Note that \( z = g \circ h(x) \), which is the output of the representation head. \( \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle \) is the cosine similarity (a measure of similarity) between features from two different pixels, whose range is limited between \(-1\) to \(1\), hence the need of temperature \( \tau \). Following [29], \( M = 50 \), \( N = 256 \), and \( \tau = 0.5 \).

    3.2. Pseudo-Labeling

    To avoid overfitting (fitting too closely) incorrect pseudo-labels, we utilize (use) entropy (a measure of uncertainty) of every pixel’s probability distribution to filter high quality pseudo-labels for further supervision. Specifically (in detail), we denote \( p_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^C \) as the softmax probabilities generated by the segmentation head of the teacher model for the \( i \)-th unlabeled image at pixel \( j \), where \( C \) is the number of classes. Its entropy is computed by:

    \[ H(p_{ij}) = -\sum_{c=0}^{C-1} p_{ij}(c) \log p_{ij}(c), (5)\]

    where \( p_{ij}(c) \) is the value of \( p_{ij} \) at \( c \)-th dimension. Then, we define pixels whose entropy on top \( \alpha_t \) as unreliable pseudo-labels at training epoch \( t \). Such unreliable pseudo-labels are not qualified for supervision. Therefore, we define the pseudo-label for the \( i \)-th unlabeled image at pixel \( j \) as:

    \[ \hat{y}_{uij} = \begin{cases} \arg \max_c p_{ij}(c), & \text{if } H(p_{ij}) < \gamma_t \\ \text{ignore}, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} (6)\]

    Figure 3. An overview of our proposed U^2 PL method. U^2 PL contains a student network and a teacher network, where the teacher is momentum-updated with the student. Labeled data is directly fed into the student network for supervised training. Given an unlabeled image, we first use the teacher model to make a prediction, and then separate the pixels into reliable ones and unreliable ones based on their entropy. Such a process is formulated (expressed) as Eq. (6). The reliable predictions are directly used as the pseudo-labels to advise the student, while each unreliable prediction is pushed into a category-wise (group-specific) memory bank. Pixels in each memory bank are regarded as the negative samples to the corresponding class, which is formulated as Eq. (4).

    where \( \gamma_t \) represents the entropy threshold (limit) at \( t \)-th training step. We set \( \gamma_t \) as the quantile corresponding to \( \alpha_t \) to limit unreliable pixels with top \( \alpha_t \) entropy, i.e., \( \gamma_t = \text{np.percentile}(H.\text{flatten}(), 100 \times (1 - \alpha_t)) \), where \( H \) is per-pixel entropy map. We adopt the following adjustment strategies in the pseudo-labeling process for better performance. Dynamic Partition Adjustment. During the training procedure, the pseudo-labels tend to be reliable gradually. Base on this intuition (natural feeling), we adjust unreliable pixels’ proportion \( \alpha_t \) with linear strategy every epoch:

    \[ \alpha_t = \alpha_0 \cdot (1 - \frac{t}{\text{total epoch}}), (7)\]

    where \( \alpha_0 \) is the initial proportion and is set to 20%, and \( t \) is the current training epoch. Adaptive Weight Adjustment. After obtaining reliable pseudo-labels, we involve them in the unsupervised loss in Eq. (3). The weight \( \lambda_u \) for this loss is defined as the reciprocal of the percentage of pixels with entropy smaller than threshold \( \gamma_t \) in the current mini-batch multiplied by a base weight \( \eta \):

    \[ \lambda_u = \eta \cdot \left( \frac{|B_u| \times H \times W}{\sum_{i=1}^{|B_u|} \sum_{j=1}^{H \times W} 1(\hat{y}_{uij} \neq \text{ignore})} \right), (8)\]

    where \( 1(\cdot) \) is the indicator function and \( \eta \) is set to 1.

    3.3. Using Unreliable Pseudo-Labels

    In semi-supervised learning tasks, discarding unreliable pseudo-labels or reducing their weights is widely used to prevent degradation (decline) of model’s performance [5, 6, 38, 42, 44, 51]. We follow this intuition (natural feeling) by filtering out unreliable pseudo-labels based on Eq. (6). However, such contempt (disregard) for unreliable pseudo-labels may result in information loss. It is obvious that unreliable pseudo-labels can provide information for better discrimination (distinction). For example, the white cross in Fig. 2, is a typical (common) unreliable pixel. Its distribution demonstrates (shows) model’s uncertainty (doubt) to distinguish between class person and class motorbike. However, this distribution also demonstrates model’s certainty (surety) to not to discriminate this pixel as class car, class train, class bicycle and so on. Such characteristic (feature) gives us the main insight (idea) to propose our U^2 PL to use unreliable pseudo-labels for semi-supervised semantic segmentation. U^2 PL, with a goal to use the information of unreliable pseudo-labels for better discrimination, coincides with recent popular contrastive learning paradigm (model) in distinguishing representation. But due to the lack of labeled images in semi-supervised semantic segmentation tasks, our U^2 PL is built on more complicated strategies. U^2 PL has three components, named anchor pixels, positive candidates and negative candidates. These components are obtained in a sampling manner from certain sets to alleviate (reduce) huge computational cost. Next, we will introduce how to selecting: (a) anchor pixels (queries); (b) positive samples for each anchor; (c) negative samples for each anchor.

    Anchor Pixels. During training, we sample anchor pixels (queries) for each class that appears in the current mini batch. We denote the set of features of all labeled candidate anchor pixels for class \( c \) as \( A_l^c \),

    \[ A_l^c = \{z_{ij} \mid y_{ij} = c, p_{ij}(c) > \delta_p\}, (9)\]

    where \( y_{ij} \) is the ground-truth for the \( j \)-th pixel of labeled image \( i \), and \( \delta_p \) denotes the positive threshold (limit) for a particular class and is set to 0.3 following [29]. \( z_{ij} \) means the representation of the \( j \)-th pixel of labeled image \( i \). For unlabeled data, counterpart \( A_u^c \) can be computed as:

    \[ A_u^c = \{z_{ij} \mid \hat{y}_{ij} = c, p_{ij}(c) > \delta_p\}. (10)\]

    It is similar to \( A_l^c \), and the only difference is that we use pseudo-label \( \hat{y}_{ij} \) based on Eq. (6) rather than hand-annotated label, which implies (suggests) that qualified anchor pixels are reliable, i.e., \( H(p_{ij}) \leq \gamma_t \). Therefore, for class \( c \), the set of all qualified anchors is

    \[ A^c = A_l^c \cup A_u^c. (11)\]

    Positive Samples. The positive sample is the same for all anchors from the same class. It is the center of all possible anchors:

    \[ z^+_c = \frac{1}{|A^c|} \sum_{z_c \in A^c} z_c. (12)\]

    Negative Samples. We define a binary variable \( n_{ij}(c) \) to identify whether the \( j \)-th pixel of image \( i \) is qualified to be negative samples of class \( c \).

    \[ n_{ij}(c) = \begin{cases} n^l_{ij}(c), & \text{if image } i \text{ is labeled} \\ n^u_{ij}(c), & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} (13)\]

    where \( n^l_{ij}(c) \) and \( n^u_{ij}(c) \) are indicators of whether the \( j \)-th pixel of labeled and unlabeled image \( i \) is qualified to be negative samples of class \( c \) respectively. For \( i \)-th labeled image, a qualified negative sample for class \( c \) should be: (a) not belonging to class \( c \); (b) difficult to distinguish between class \( c \) and its ground-truth category. Therefore, we introduce the pixel-level category order \( O_{ij} = \text{argsort}(p_{ij}) \). Obviously (clearly), we have \( O_{ij}(\arg \max p_{ij}) = 0 \) and \( O_{ij}(\arg \min p_{ij}) = C - 1 \).

    \[ n^l_{ij}(c) = 1 [y_{ij} \neq c] \cdot 1 [0 \leq O_{ij}(c) < r_l], (14)\]

    where \( r_l \) is the low rank threshold (limit) and is set to 3. The two indicators reflect feature (a) and (b) respectively. For \( i \)-th unlabeled image, a qualified negative sample for class \( c \) should: (a) be unreliable; (b) probably not belongs to class \( c \); (c) not belongs to most unlikely (least probable) classes. Similarly (in the same way), we also use \( O_{ij} \) to define \( n^u_{ij}(c) \):

    \[ n^u_{ij}(c) = 1 [H(p_{ij}) > \gamma_t] \cdot 1 [r_l \leq O_{ij}(c) < r_h], (15) \]

    where \( r_h \) is the high rank threshold and is set to 20. Finally, the set of negative samples of class \( c \) is

    \[ N^c = \{z_{ij} \mid n_{ij}(c) = 1\}. (16)\]

    Category-wise Memory Bank. Due to the long tail phenomenon of the dataset, negative candidates in some

In order to maintain a stable number of negative samples, we use category-wise (group-specific) memory bank \( Q^c \) (FIFO queue) to store the negative samples for class \( c \).     4. Experiments

    4.1. Setup

    Datasets. PASCAL VOC 2012 [16] Dataset is a standard semantic segmentation benchmark (test) with 20 semantic classes of objects and 1 class of background. The training set and the validation set include 1,464 and 1,449 images respectively. Following [11, 23, 44], we use SBD [20] as the augmented set with 9,118 additional training images. Since the SBD [20] dataset is coarsely annotated (labeled), PseudoSeg [51] takes only the standard 1,464 images as the whole labeled set, while other methods [11, 23] take all 10,582 images as candidate labeled data. Therefore, we evaluate (assess) our method on both the classic set (1,464 candidate labeled images) and the blender set (10,582 candidate labeled images). Cityscapes [12], a dataset designed for urban scene understanding, consists of 2,975 training images with fine-annotated masks and 500 validation images. For each dataset, we compare U^2 PL with other methods under 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, and 1/16 partition protocols.

    Network Structure. We use ResNet-101 [21] pre-trained on ImageNet [13] as the backbone and DeepLabv3+ [8] as the decoder. Both of the segmentation head and the representation head consists of two Conv-BN-ReLU blocks, where both blocks preserve the feature map resolution and the first block halves the number of channels. The segmentation head can be seen as a pixel-level classifier, mapping the 512 dimensional features output from ASPP module into \( C \) classes. The representation head maps the same features into 256 dimensional representation space.

Note that when labeled data is extremely limited, e.g., when we only have 92 labeled data, our U^2 PL outperforms previous methods by a large margin (+10.98% under 1/16 split for classic PASCAL VOC 2012), proofing the efficiency of using unreliable pseudo-labels.

    4.4. Qualitative Results

    Fig. 4 shows the results of different methods on the PASCAL VOC 2012 val set. Benefiting from using unreliable pseudo-labels, U^2 PL outperforms other methods. Note that using contrastive learning without filtering those unreliable pixels, sometimes does harm to the model (see row 2 and row 4 in Fig. 4), leading to worse results than those when the model is trained only by labeled data.

    Furthermore, through visualizing the segmentation results, we find that our method achieves much better performance on those ambiguous regions (e.g., the border between different objects). Such visual difference proves that our method finally makes the reliability of unreliable prediction labels stronger.

    5. Conclusion

    We propose a semi-supervised semantic segmentation framework U^2 PL by including unreliable pseudo-labels into training, which outperforms many existing state-of-the-art methods, suggesting our framework provide a new promising paradigm in semi-supervised learning research. Our ablation experiments proves the insight of this work is quite solid. Qualitative result gives a visual proof for its effectiveness, especially the better performance on borders between semantic objects or other ambiguous regions.

    The training of our method is time-consuming compared with fully-supervised methods [7, 8, 30, 36, 47], which is a common disadvantage for semi-supervised learning tasks [11, 22, 23, 34, 44, 49]. Due to the extreme lack of labels, the semi-supervised learning frameworks commonly need to pay a price in time for higher accuracy.
